# Predicting Kit End Dates: Dell Data Science Case Study

**Zakir Patwa · 2026**

This project predicts when kitting finishes (`kit_end`) for 12,069 upcoming orders (kit starts Feb 3 – Mar 7, 2026), using 177,727 cleaned historical orders. A tuned LightGBM model puts **86.1%** of held-out orders within ±1 day of the true finish date, compared with **80.6%** for the best simple rule. It also gives each order a **risk score** that finds slow orders at **3× the rate of random selection**.

The analysis also shows *why* the remaining errors happen: slowdowns hit one product family at a time, in bursts, the way part shortages behave. The data has no record of part availability or warehouse space. **Recording those two things is the single biggest improvement available**, bigger than any change to the algorithm.

## 1. Problem and approach

| Decision | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Predict **calendar days** from kit start to kit end, then add them to `kit_start` | `kit_start` is known for every inference order, so duration is the only unknown, and duration patterns carry over across months |
| Model | Built both a LightGBM **classifier** (0, 1, 2, 3, 4+ days) and a **regressor**; kept the better one | The regressor (L1 / absolute-error objective) won; the classifier supplies probabilities for the risk score |
| Validation | **Time-based**: fit on Mar–Nov 2025, test on Dec 2025 – Jan 2026 | Inference is in the future, so validation must be too; a random split would leak future information |
| Baselines | Always 1 day · median per product family · median per family × warehouse | The model must beat rules a planner could run in a spreadsheet |
| Metrics | **Within ±1 day (primary)**, exact day, mean absolute error (MAE), macro-F1; for the risk score: log loss, calibration, precision/recall | ±1 day is what a planner can schedule around; probability checks show whether the risk score can be trusted |
| Output | `.npy` of `datetime64[D]` dates, with asserts on length, completeness and row order | Loads with a plain `np.load` |

Tuning used a separate inner split (fit Mar–Oct, check November), so the Dec–Jan test months were never used to choose settings. All scores use true, uncapped kit days.

## 2. What drives kitting time: three hypotheses

The analysis starts from a supply-chain view: kitting is fast when **parts are in stock**, the **warehouse has room**, and **people's schedules** don't get in the way. Each idea was tested with the closest data available.

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **H1. Supply constraints** | **Supported (indirectly)** | Families differ sharply in how often they run long (0% to 66.5% of orders taking 4+ days, among families with 200+ orders). Slowdowns come in **family-specific bursts**: 83 family-weeks where one family runs far slower than the plant hold **31% of all slow orders**. |
| **H2. Human schedules** | **Supported** | Orders started on **Saturday average 2.08 days vs 1.02 on Wednesday** (Sunday is rarely worked). A holiday within 3 days **doubles** the slow-order share (10.4% vs 4.8%). Overtime can't be separated cleanly (83% of days have it). |
| **H3. Warehouse congestion** | **Can't tell** | Warehouse 3JU looks slower than KHO (1.58 vs 1.28 days), but **for the same products the gap disappears**: 3JU simply handles slower families. There is no data on space or occupancy. |

![Weekly share of slow orders by product family](figures/h1_family_week_heatmap.png)

*Each row is a high-volume product family and each column a week; darker means more orders took 4+ days. Vertical stripes are plant-wide (holidays). Isolated dark cells are one family slowing down while the rest of the plant runs normally, which is the pattern a part shortage produces.*

## 3. Data issues and how they were handled

| Issue | Rows | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| `kit_end` before `kit_start` | 734 train | **Dropped.** 712 sit on three start dates (e.g. 562 of 1,265 orders on 2025-07-08), so the start date looks re-stamped; the true start is unrecoverable |
| Extract filtered on `kit_end` ≥ 2025-03-10 | 1,392 train | **Dropped** kit starts before 2025-03-10. Fast early-March orders were missing, so those days were 78–100% slow. Left in, this faked an early-March crisis and made one warehouse look slow |
| Numbers stored as text | all | Cast to integers |
| `est_ship_date` = 1900-01-01 placeholder | 28 train / 1 inference | Set to missing + flag |
| `order_amt` = 0 | 2,049 train / 100 inference | Set to missing + flag |
| `capacity` repeated rows | 191,934 → 317 dates | One row per date. Dates *missing* from the table (Sundays, holidays, a Feb 6–7, 2026 closure) are treated as non-working days |
| `expected_minutes` covers 166 of 360 families | ~25% train / ~20% inference rows | Fallback to business-line median, then global median, with a flag for the source |
| Repeated (order, family) keys | 10 pairs | **Kept**: each pair differs in `tie_num` and `line_qty`, so they are separate sub-lines |

Training: 179,853 → 177,727 rows (−1.2%). Inference: all 12,069 rows kept in original order, enforced by asserts. The notebook's **Assumptions log** lists all 24 assumptions.

**Leakage checks.** Family-history features (e.g. a family's average kit days) are learned only from the training months, and each training row's value is computed from the *other* training rows (out-of-fold), so no row sees its own answer. The estimated ship date was **removed** as a feature: it could be revised after kitting starts. Removing it changed accuracy by only 0.4 points.

## 4. Results

Validation: 33,377 orders with kit starts Dec 1, 2025 – Jan 31, 2026.

| Model | Within ±1 day | Exact day | MAE (days) | Macro-F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline: always 1 day | 79.8% | 46.9% | 0.918 | 0.128 |
| Baseline: family median | 80.6% | 45.8% | 0.909 | 0.159 |
| Baseline: family × warehouse median | 80.4% | 45.4% | 0.915 | 0.161 |
| LightGBM classifier (±1-optimal decision rule) | 85.8% | 48.3% | 0.812 | 0.197 |
| **LightGBM regressor (selected)** | **86.1%** | **51.5%** | **0.780** | **0.249** |

![Model comparison on validation](figures/f1_model_comparison.png)

- **+5.5 points within ±1 day and −14% MAE** vs the best baseline. The family medians barely beat "always 1 day", because nearly every family's median is 1. The family signal is in the slow tail, and the model captures it.
- The classifier and regressor are **statistically tied** on ±1-day accuracy (a bootstrap over kit-start days puts the gap between −2.4 and +1.4 points). The regressor was chosen for its lower MAE.
- The model relies about equally on **schedule and capacity** (43.7% of importance: next day working, weekday, fiscal week, overtime) and **product** (43.0%). Warehouse contributes 0.7%.
- **Limit:** the model mostly predicts 1 or 2 days and rarely predicts 4+. Nothing in the data reliably signals in advance which order will run long. The **risk score** covers that gap.

**Risk score (probability of 4+ days).** It beats historical frequencies on log loss by 11.7%. On validation, flagging the top ~10% of orders (threshold set on November) caught **37% of slow orders at 25% precision, 3.0× the base rate**. It ranks well but runs low in absolute terms (5.0% predicted vs 8.4% actual), so it should be used as a **ranking**. For inference, the flag marks the riskiest 10% of orders.

## 5. Why errors remain: evidence of missing information

![Error analysis](figures/f5_error_analysis.png)

- **Slow orders drive the error.** Orders taking 4+ days are 8.4% of orders but **40% of total error**, and **93% of large misses are orders that took longer than predicted**.
- **Misses cluster by product and week.** 40 family-weeks holding 8.5% of orders contain **38% of all misses**. This is the same burst pattern as H1.
- **Identical-looking orders still differ.** For orders sharing family, warehouse *and* start date, **a third of the variation in kit days is within those groups**, and 22% of orders sit in a group whose kit days span 3+ days. Every recorded input is identical within a group, so the cause isn't in the data.
- **Ceiling.** Even an impossible model that knew each group's actual median outcome would reach only **93.4%** within ±1 day (vs 86.2% for the model on those orders). Closing the gap requires information that doesn't exist in these tables.

## 6. Recommendations

1. **Record part availability per order.** For example, a flag or count of short or backordered components at kit start, and shortage events by part. This is the most direct fix for the largest error source: slowdowns hit **one family at a time** (31% of slow orders in family-specific bursts, 38% of model misses in 8.5% of orders), which the current data can't anticipate. A shortage flag would let the model, and planners, see a slow order coming instead of explaining it afterwards.

2. **Record daily warehouse capacity and occupancy** (staging space used, open kits in progress, headcount per shift) for each warehouse. Congestion (H3) currently **can't be tested**. 3JU and 3JHF miss about 22% of orders vs about 10% at KHO, but the data can't say whether that's space, staffing or product mix. Occupancy data would settle it and give the model a second missing signal.

3. **Use the risk score to triage each batch.** Give planners a daily list of the **top 10% riskiest orders** (the `high_risk_flag` column in the planner CSV). On validation that list was 3× richer in slow orders than a random pick and caught 37% of them, which is enough to target expediting, part checks or customer communication at a manageable workload.

4. **Plan around the calendar.** Orders started on **Saturday or the day before a closure take about 1 extra calendar day** (Saturday 2.08 vs Wednesday 1.02 days; before a closed day, 2.36 vs 1.07 predicted). Where ship dates are tight, avoid starting kits right before non-working days, or build the extra day into promise dates. The Feb 6–7, 2026 closure affects 1,442 inference orders.

5. **Fix three data-capture issues:** `kit_start` being overwritten for batches of orders (734 rows ended before they started); the extract filtering on `kit_end` (which biases the earliest weeks); and missing **timestamps on `est_ship_date` changes**, which would show whether it is safe to use as a feature (it was excluded here as a precaution).

## 7. Limitations and assumptions

- **The tail stays hard to predict.** The model is built for the typical order; individual 4+ day orders are flagged by risk, not predicted exactly.
- **Risk probabilities run low** (fit on months with fewer slow orders). Use them as a ranking, and re-check calibration on recent data before quoting them as percentages.
- **Validation months include holidays**; the inference window has none. Accuracy on inference may be somewhat better than validation, but that can't be verified without the true dates.
- **Inference may be a subset of real volume** (431 vs 626 orders/day), so daily order volume was not used as a feature. Re-stamped start dates like those found in training can't be detected in inference.
- The calendar (site `FCJ`) is assumed to apply to the order site `KHO`; overtime hours are assumed to be planned in advance. Product and business-line names are obfuscated in the source data.
